Model and why "exactly 1" fails

Vertices i, j ∈ V, Euclidean distance dij, coefficient aij = 1 if dij ≤ R (so aii = 1), decision xj ∈ {0,1} = open a facility at j.

min  Σ_j x_j
s.t. Σ_j a_ij x_j  ≥ 1   for every vertex i     (set cover)
     Σ_j a_ij x_j  = 1   for every vertex i     (exact cover variant)
     x_j ∈ {0,1}

Why "= 1" goes wrong. With the equality, two open facilities can never both reach the same vertex. That rules out every overlap. On most layouts some vertex sits where any facility covering it overlaps another facility's circle, so the model becomes infeasible. In graph terms, "= 1" asks for a perfect code (efficient dominating set), and most graphs don't have one.

It can never just "cost more". When the candidate sites are the vertices themselves, a feasible exact cover is always also a minimum cover. Suppose E covers every vertex exactly once. Then the neighbourhoods N[e] for e ∈ E are disjoint. Any feasible cover D must cover every e ∈ E, so it needs a vertex inside each N[e]. Hence |D| ≥ |E|. So "= 1" either gives the same answer or no answer at all. A higher cost is only possible when candidate sites differ from demand points.

Why eyeballing fails. The instances from "Find tricky instance" are selected so that the greedy rule ("always open the facility covering the most uncovered vertices") is not optimal. The obvious choice is a trap. Optimality is proven by an exact branch-and-bound solver that runs in your browser.
